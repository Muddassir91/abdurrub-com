## Part 1: Ben Settle openings

Ben Settle (Email Players) writes daily emails built on "infotainment": entertain first, teach second, sell every time. His openings are short, odd and personal. They never give the content away in the subject line, and the first line pulls the reader into a scene or an argument before any teaching starts.

Use these patterns. Mark any opening built from them as "(Settle)" when presenting options.

### Subject line patterns

1. **The odd, specific, half-sentence.** A phrase that makes no sense until you read the email. Example shape: "the goat that ate my Tuesday".
2. **The pop-culture or villain hook.** A character, film, wrestler or news villain used as a doorway into the lesson. Example shape: "What Darth Vader knew about retention".
3. **The confession.** Admitting a mistake or embarrassing moment. Example shape: "I nearly lost this client last week".
4. **The quote lead.** A striking line someone said to him, in quotation marks. Example shape: "'Your emails are too long'".
5. **The question that bothers them.** A direct question aimed at a fear they already have. Example shape: "Why do your flows stop making money after week 2?".
6. **The lowercase, one-to-three-word teaser.** Looks like a note from a friend, not a campaign. Example shape: "weird", "about yesterday", "bad news".
7. **The contrarian flip.** Taking a position against the accepted advice. Example shape: "Stop writing welcome emails".
8. **The reader mailbag.** Framing the email as a reply to a reader. Example shape: "A reader asks about discount codes".
9. **The number with a twist.** A number that is suspiciously specific or deliberately unimpressive. Example shape: "The 3 word subject line that out-pulled everything".
10. **The time or place anchor.** Starting mid-scene with a time, place or mundane detail. Example shape: "Sunday, 6:12am, Lisbon".

### First-line patterns

1. **In medias res.** Open in the middle of a scene. "So there I was, standing in a Shopify dashboard at midnight..."
2. **The mundane-to-lesson bridge.** Something trivial from daily life (a dog, a drive, a bad meal) that turns into the marketing point.
3. **The reader quote.** "Got this email from a reader yesterday:" then the quote, then the response.
4. **The contrarian declaration.** One sentence that picks a fight with the industry.
5. **The story in progress.** "Quick story." or "True story:" followed by a short anecdote.
6. **The confession opener.** "I have a confession."
7. **The 'here is what happened' recap.** Reporting a conversation, a client result or a screwup like a diary entry.
8. **The pre-frame.** "This will annoy some people." or "Not for everyone, but..."
9. **The one-word paragraph.** A single word on its own line, then the story.
10. **The direct address.** Speaking to one reader about one problem they have right now.

### Settle rules of thumb

- Subject line and first line should not repeat each other.
- Never explain the subject line in the first line; make them open to find out.
- Entertain before you educate. The lesson arrives late and the pitch arrives last.
- One idea per email. The opening sets up exactly one tension.
- Personality over polish. Odd beats clever.

## Part 2: The drama tabloid method (from Muddassir and Matthew Volkwyn)

Muddassir Abdur Rub and Matthew Volkwyn brainstormed an easy, completely overlooked method to source winning ideas for drama story ads. It has already cranked out multiple top spenders for 8 and 9 figure brands.

**Just look at drama media outlets and tabloids.**

That industry has been around far longer than ecom and spent over a century perfecting the art of hooking and retaining attention with diabolical headlines and stories. Take those headlines and stories and turn them into drama ads.

Credit: Michael Kleynhans for sharing the example pics that accompanied the original post.

### Outlets to check (US and UK)

| Outlet | Section to check | URL |
|---|---|---|
| Take a Break | Real life | https://www.takeabreak.co.uk/real-life/ |
| That's Life! | Real life stories | https://www.thatslife.co.uk |
| Chat | Real life | https://www.chatmagazine.co.uk |
| The Sun | Fabulous, real life | https://www.thesun.co.uk/fabulous/real-life/ |
| Daily Mirror | Real life stories | https://www.mirror.co.uk/news/real-life-stories/ |
| Daily Mail | Femail | https://www.dailymail.co.uk/femail/index.html |
| New York Post | Relationships tag | https://nypost.com/tag/relationships/ |
| National Enquirer | Homepage | https://www.nationalenquirer.com |
| TMZ | Homepage | https://www.tmz.com |
| E! News | Homepage | https://www.eonline.com |

### How to use it every time openings are requested

1. Fetch the current headlines from as many of the outlets above as possible (web search or fetch). Grab the 20 to 30 strongest.
2. Pull out the structure of each headline: the trigger (betrayal, secret, transformation, revenge, shock reveal, "I was wrong", money, health scare, family drama), the specificity (ages, amounts, time spans, place names), and the tension device (withheld detail, contradiction, confession).
3. Map the offer at hand onto those structures. Keep the emotional shape, swap in the product's world. The story should feel like it came from a tabloid real-life page, not an ad.
4. Present the adapted openings alongside the original tabloid headline or pattern they came from, so Muddassir can judge the fit.
5. For drama story ads, go beyond the headline: steal the story arc too (setup, humiliation or crisis, discovery, turning point, outcome, reflection), then write the ad as that arc.

### Tabloid headline patterns to fall back on if the web is unavailable

- "I [did something shocking] and [unexpected consequence]"
- "My [relative] [betrayed / shocked] me with [specific detail], then [twist]"
- "[Age]-year-old [ordinary person] reveals [secret] that [result]"
- "I spent [amount] on [thing] and [regret or triumph]"
- "Doctors told me [bad news], then [discovery]"
- "I was [humiliated] at [event], so I [response]"
- "Everyone said [common belief]. I found out the truth."
- "[Person] thought they were [state]. One [small thing] changed everything."
- "The [item] in my [place] that [consequence]"
- "I left [thing] for [other thing] and I have never looked back"
